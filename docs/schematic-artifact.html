Rudder angle indicator — full schematic

ESP32-C3 Pro Mini + ADS1115, powered from a 12–24V DC bus of unknown polarity through a bridge-rectifier input stage and an LM2596 buck. Two independent regulated rails: +5V runs the Pro Mini (self-regulating) and biases the float-sensor divider; +3.3V (AMS1117) feeds AVDD — a ferrite-isolated analog supply dedicated to the ADC and the Hall sensor's ratiometric supply, sharing one unbroken ground plane with the rest of the board.

Sheet 1 — Power input & LM2596 buck (12/24V → 5V)

RA-1–01 · Rev G
  • Why a bridge instead of the MOSFET. Using DC on a bridge rectifier's AC terminals is a standard trick: two of the four diodes always conduct, so the output is correct-polarity no matter which way J1 is wired. Unlike the earlier series-MOSFET protection, which simply blocks power on a reversed connection, the bridge keeps the indicator working even if someone wires it backwards — worth deciding whether that's what you want for a helm instrument versus a hard fail that makes a wiring mistake obvious.
  • The cost is voltage drop, not complexity. Two diodes conduct in series at any time, so BR1 drops roughly 2×Vf ≈ 1.4–2V (standard silicon) continuously — negligible headroom loss on 24V, but worth checking on a 12V system during engine-cranking sag (LM2596 still regulates fine down to a few volts above 5V, so there's margin, just less of it).
  • Thermal. At an estimated few hundred mA total system draw, a W10 DIP package runs cool with no heatsink. If your real current draw creeps toward 1A continuous, consider a Schottky bridge (e.g. GBU-series) instead — roughly half the voltage drop and dissipation of a silicon bridge like the W10.
  • Fuse placement. F1 only needs to sit in series with one leg (here, leg A, ahead of BR1's AC1 terminal) — a single fuse still protects the loop regardless of which physical wire ends up carrying current.
  • D1 TVS selection. Use a 33V-clamp part (e.g. SMBJ33A) on 24V systems, or an 18V part on 12V-only systems — size it to survive alternator load-dump without clamping normal operating voltage. It's unidirectional now since BR1 fixes the polarity ahead of it.
  • L1 selection. Pick inductance from the LM2596 datasheet's Vin/Vout/Iload chart, sized for the combined load: Pro Mini Wi-Fi peaks + AMS1117 input current + float-divider current. Use a shielded type to limit radiated EMI near the analog front end on Sheet 4.
  • U1 package note. Confirm exact pin numbering against the datasheet for your chosen package (TO-220-5 IC vs. a drop-in module).

Sheet 2 — AMS1117-3.3 LDO & rail distribution (5V → 3.3V)

RA-1–02 · Rev G
  • Why not run the LM2596 straight to 3.3V? The AMS1117's high PSRR filters the buck's switching ripple before it reaches the 16-bit ADC reference — important given the sub-mV resolution you're after.
  • FB3: yes, worth adding. A ferrite bead between the shared 3.3V node and AVDD (the ADS1115 + Hall sensor rail) is a standard, low-risk way to block any remaining high-frequency noise from ever reaching the analog supply. C6 gives the bead's output a local high-frequency return path right where it matters.
  • A matching ground-side ferrite (FB4): deliberately left out. Splitting the ground plane with a bead is a real technique, but it's a much bigger risk on a 2-layer board, which has only one copper pour doing double duty as the return path for every signal. Any trace that ends up crossing the split forces its return current the long way around through the bead, which can make EMI and noise coupling worse, not better. This board gets the isolation benefit from FB3 alone, plus keeping the ADS1115/sensor front-end physically grouped away from the LM2596 and ESP32-C3 on a single, unbroken ground pour (Sheet 8 grounding notes in the companion doc). If you later move to 4 layers with a dedicated ground plane, a ground-side split becomes much safer to revisit.
  • Light load, no heatsinking needed. With the Pro Mini off this rail, U2 only feeds the ADS1115 (~150µA) and the Hall sensor's excitation current (typically well under 50mA) — dissipation is negligible.
  • Two independent 3.3V domains. This rail and the Pro Mini's own internal 3.3V (from its onboard regulator, Sheet 3) are separate regulators at the same nominal voltage, not the same net — see Sheet 3 notes on why that's fine for I²C.

Sheet 3 — ESP32-C3 Pro Mini

RA-1–03 · Rev G
  • Why 5V, not 3.3V. The Pro Mini's onboard regulator expects 5V (or similar) on its VIN/5V pin and produces its own internal 3.3V for the chip — feeding it the AMS1117's 3.3V would bypass that regulator and is not how this board is designed to be powered.
  • Two independent 3.3V domains. The Pro Mini's internal 3.3V and the AMS1117's 3.3V (Sheet 2, feeding the ADS1115) are separate regulators at the same nominal voltage — not the same net. They share only GND. Reference the I²C pull-ups (Sheet 4) to the ADS1115's 3.3V rail, not the Pro Mini's; standard I²C works fine across the two domains as long as ground is common.
  • ALERT/RDY. Wiring the ADS1115's ALERT pin to a spare GPIO lets firmware wait on conversion-ready via interrupt instead of a fixed polling delay across 4 channels.
  • Strapping pins. Avoid GPIO9 and other boot-mode strap pins for I²C/ALERT if your specific Pro Mini variant doesn't already keep them clear.

Sheet 4 — ADS1115 & sensor interface

RA-1–04 · Rev G
  • Why AIN2/AIN3 have no connector. Earlier revisions of this design ran dedicated Kelvin-sense wires all the way to the Hall sensor's own supply/ground pins to cancel cable IR drop. Since the sensor only has 3 pins (GND, VCC, SIG) and there's no spare wire for that, AIN2/AIN3 instead read the board's own AVDD and GND directly — simpler wiring, and still catches the two things that actually matter here: AVDD regulator drift/tolerance (the Hall sensor's VCC is the same net, so this is what its ratiometric output is really relative to) and the ADC's own offset error. What it no longer catches is IR drop in the sensor cable itself — negligible in practice given the sensor's low current draw and a reasonably short, adequately-gauged cable run.
  • No op-amp needed. The AIN1 divider's Thevenin source impedance tops out around 138Ω (at R2=190Ω) — trivial next to the ADS1115's input, and the 10kΩ anti-alias resistor already dominates. A buffer would add a part and a failure point without fixing anything real. If the tank cable run is long and noisy, an optional MCP6001 follower between the divider node and R7 is the one case worth keeping as a footprint.
  • Gain. AIN0/AIN2/AIN3 comfortably use ±4.096V (gain=1). For AIN1, whose signal only reaches ~1.4V, set a higher per-conversion PGA gain (e.g. ±2.048V) to roughly double effective resolution — free, since the ADS1115 lets you change gain per channel.
  • Mux settling. Discard the first conversion after each channel switch; the ADS1115 has one ADC core behind a single mux.
  • Confirm your sender's curve. R2 = 0–190Ω matches a common European/VDO-style fuel or water sender; some senders run 0–90Ω or reversed — check yours before committing to R1 = 510Ω.
  • Cable practice. J2 (3-conductor) and J3 (2-conductor) as twisted pairs, shielded and grounded at the electronics end only, routed clear of the LM2596 switching loop on Sheet 1. Neither needs the 5-conductor run a Kelvin-sense design would have required.

Default passive size: 0805. Resistors and small ceramic caps default to 0805 throughout — hand-solder-friendly on a 2-layer board while still keeping things compact. Drop to 0603 across the board if you want a tighter layout and you're reflow-soldering (oven or hot-air), not hand-soldering with an iron.
Two parts substituted for SMD. BR1 (bridge rectifier) and D2 (catch diode) were specified by their classic THT part numbers (W10, 1N5822) earlier in this design purely by function/rating — for this SMD build, use their SMD-package equivalents (DF10S-class bridge, SS54 Schottky) called out in the Package column above. Everything else was already SMD-native.
Before you lay this out: confirm exact pin numbering for U1, U2, U3, U4 against the datasheet for the specific package/module you buy — this drawing set fixes pin function and net topology, which is what the electrical design depends on; physical pin numbers are a footprint detail you'll cross-reference during PCB layout.
